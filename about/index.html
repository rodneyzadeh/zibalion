Rodney Zadeh

I have always been more interested in structure than appearance.

Taking things apart is my default. Objects, systems, claims, motives, arrangements, explanations. I want to know what holds a thing together, what it depends on, and what remains when one of its assumptions is removed.

It is less a discipline than a habit of mind.

I became interested early in the distance between what people say, what they mean, and what they do. We often treat the three as though they belong naturally together. They do not. Much of human behavior becomes legible only in the space between them.

For a long time, that attention went into art.

I drew, painted, sculpted, wrote poetry and songs, moving from one form to another in search of something capable of carrying what I was trying to understand.

Life drawing made attention exact.

When you draw from a model, the difficulty is not that you do not know what a hand looks like. The difficulty is that you do.

The task is to see this hand.

The moment the general idea replaces the particular thing in front of you, the drawing begins to fail. The error is not merely technical. It is epistemic. Knowledge has become interference.

Magic taught me the inverse problem.

A magician does not need to conceal the whole event. He needs only to shape the audience's account of what happened.

We notice selectively. We infer continuity. We complete what is incomplete. Given a sufficiently coherent explanation, we often stop looking for another one.

Magic depends on that surrender.

Art taught me to question the assumptions I bring to perception.

Magic taught me how easily those assumptions can be furnished from outside.

Together they became a method:

Look carefully.

Then ask what your way of looking has already decided for you.

I spent years running businesses, including a bookselling business for nearly a decade. I never stopped buying books or reading them.

Business gave these questions consequences.

Incentives ceased to be abstractions. Principles behave one way in speech and another under pressure from money, scarcity, status, responsibility, risk, and time.

What people say matters.

What they choose when the choice becomes costly often matters more.

Eventually I found programming.

Code interested me because it occupies a peculiar territory between thought and force.

An idea can become a rule. A preference can become a default. An assumption can be embedded in a system and repeated millions of times without ever having to present itself again as an assumption.

An assumption in the mind is still only an idea.

An assumption embedded in a system becomes a condition.

Other people then have to live inside it.

That distinction sits at the center of most of what I write about.

I'm still learning to build. I expect I always will be.

This site is where I examine the things I find difficult to leave alone: technology, interfaces, incentives, business models, systems, and the assumptions buried inside them.

I tend to return to the same questions.

What is this actually doing?

What must its makers believe about the person using it?

What has been made easy?

What has been made difficult?

And where have the costs been moved so that they disappear from view?

I have little interest in performing certainty.

Certainty can be the result of serious inquiry. It can also be what appears when inquiry stops too soon.

Some questions resolve.

Others become more precise.

Precision is also progress.

I build things as well. Software, hardware, experiments, prototypes that work and prototypes that don't.

Building is part of the argument.

Criticism can identify what is wrong with an arrangement. Construction asks a more difficult question:

Was it necessary?

Sometimes what looks inevitable is only familiar.

Sometimes what looks like a constraint is only a convention nobody has yet bothered to challenge.

That is where building becomes philosophically useful. It forces an idea to encounter resistance.

A principle that survives construction has earned the right to be taken more seriously.

A principle that does not has taught you something.

So this site is partly a record of what I read, partly of what I build, and partly of what becomes visible when ordinary things are taken apart.

I am interested in the point where an idea becomes a rule, where a rule becomes an environment, and where an environment begins shaping the people who live inside it.

If I get something wrong, tell me.

An argument worth making should be able to survive correction.
