How I work

Everything on this site starts on paper and ends on a screen. The distance between those two things is the method.

I am not a trained writer. I read, I take the reading apart by hand, and I argue the pieces back together until something holds.

The room I do it in is not decoration. Each station has a specific job and the jobs run in order. Books feed the cards. The cards find the question. The question becomes an essay. The bench is where the ideas that are about machines get tested against actual machines, because an idea about technology that has never touched a soldering iron is a hunch wearing a lab coat.

This page is the tour.

01

The library

The library is an environment, not a reading list. I do not work through it front to back. I keep several books open at once and let them talk to each other, which is where most of my questions actually come from. Cicero next to Weizenbaum produces things that neither one produces alone.

The photograph shows one wall. The collection runs well past what fits in a frame, and it is still growing.

I have rules about what gets on the shelf. Penguin Classics, NYRB, Oxford, Everyman. No print on demand, no AI-generated filler, no unattributed reprints. If a book is a repackaging of someone else's thinking, I would rather have the someone else. This is slow and expensive and I think it is worth both.

Every book is read with a pencil. I annotate as I go and I build an index inside the back cover as I read, so a finished book arrives at the end with its own map. That map is what I work from later, and it is the raw material for the boxes.

On the shelves right now: Roman history and the primary sources under it. Existentialism, approached through Buber before Sartre. Economics read from opposing corners on purpose, Sowell against Piketty, Friedman against Mankiw. Philosophy of technology. The history of computing. And the Culture novels, which are the reason half of what I write about abundance exists at all.

02

The zettelkasten

Two wooden boxes. Several thousand index cards. Colored tabs. It looks like a filing system from 1958 because it essentially is one.

The reason it is on paper is that paper is slow. A note-taking app lets you capture without understanding, and you end up with a warehouse of highlights you will never open again. Writing a card by hand forces me to say the thing in my own words before it is allowed into the box. If I cannot write the card, I did not understand the passage, and that failure is useful information on its own.

The shape of it comes from Scott Scheper's Antinet Zettelkasten, which makes the case for keeping the whole apparatus analog. What I took from it is the argument against categories. A card is not filed under a subject decided in advance. It goes next to whatever it continues, and the structure branches outward as the thinking does. Sorting by topic freezes your conclusions before you have any. Branching lets the box tell you later what it turned out to be about.

I do not run Scheper's system to the letter and would not claim to. He is far stricter about addressing than I am, and I have bent the rest to the way I read. But the case for paper is his, and it is the reason two wooden boxes sit on the desk instead of an app.

The output of the box is never a draft. It is a question. When enough cards start pointing at the same tension, that tension becomes the next essay, and the sources are already attached, because they are written on the cards.

03

The desk

This is where the writing and the code happen, and it is built the way it is for one reason. I want the machine to stop asking me questions.

Window management is automated. The moves I make most often are one keypress on a macropad instead of four on a keyboard. The scripts that need to run on a schedule run on a schedule, without me. None of this makes me faster in a way I could put a number on. What it does is remove the small decisions, and small decisions are what eat a working session alive.

There is a version of this setup that is pure aesthetics, and I am aware the colored light does not help me think. But nothing sits on the desk that has not earned a place there.

04

The bench

The bench is the newest station and the least finished, which is the honest description of it.

I write about technology. I did not want to be someone who writes about technology entirely from the outside, so the hardware ladder is going up in parallel with the reading. A Raspberry Pi 5 is running. A six node cluster is the next rung. Alongside it there is a rack of Android devices driven from a single terminal, which has no commercial purpose and exists because I want to know what actually breaks when you scale that.

None of it is finished. I am publishing the ladder rather than the summit deliberately. A page showing only completed projects would be a different kind of dishonesty than the one I am trying to avoid.

05

The shelves

The annotation system

Nothing goes on the shelf untouched. Every book I own is read with a pencil, and the marking happens in three layers.

The first is marginalia. I underline what lands and write the reaction beside it in my own words. Not "important" or "good point," which are useless six months later, but why it landed and what it collides with. Ugly handwriting is fine. Illegible handwriting is not.

The second is the index. The inside back cover of every book becomes a map of that book: a page number and three words, nothing more. By the last page I have an index of what mattered to me rather than what mattered to the publisher's indexer. It costs almost nothing and it is the part of the practice I would give up last.

The third is the commonplace book. At the end of a book, or once a week, the few passages that survived get copied out by hand into a notebook with the date, the source, the page, and a line on why. Copying by hand is the whole point. It is slow, and the slowness is what makes the sentence yours. Typing it does not.

Tabs go only on reference books, the ones I return to for a specific chapter. Novels never get them. That is what the back cover is for. I keep a running list of words I did not know, which is less a vocabulary exercise than a record of where my reading outran me.

This is what feeds the boxes. A passage has to survive the pencil, then the index, then the notebook, before it is worth a card. Most do not.

What follows is an index of the library, grouped by subject. Two honest notes before you open it. I have not read all of these. Some I have worked through closely, some I have read in fragments, and some are waiting. And this is not the whole room either. It is what I have got written down, which is a different thing from what is on the shelves.

Recommend a book

If something in that index suggests a book I am missing, tell me. I am most interested in the ones I would not find on my own, and in the case for why rather than the title alone. I read everything that comes in. I do not reply to everything.

Sending this adds you to my list, so I can reply and so you get new pieces when they are finished. No tracking pixels, the list is never sold, and unsubscribing takes one click.
